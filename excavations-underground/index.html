Excavations & Underground in Dublin

Site investigations you can build on.

LEARN MORE

Dublin's subsurface is dominated by the Dublin Boulder Clay, a dense lodgement till deposited during the last glaciation. This formation, typically 15 to 40 meters thick across the city, contains cobbles and boulders in a stiff sandy silt matrix. Anchor design here cannot rely on generic rock or soil assumptions. The material behaves as an overconsolidated soil with significant locked-in horizontal stresses, which directly influences both active and passive anchor capacity. A detailed site investigation with test pits is essential to identify boulder fields before anchor installation begins. The Liffey valley adds further complexity, where alluvial gravels overlying the till create variable groundwater conditions that affect bond zone selection. Each anchor in Dublin must be designed with full awareness of these glacial legacy conditions.

Anchors in Dublin Boulder Clay routinely achieve bond stresses of 300–450 kPa in the stiff till matrix, but values can drop below 100 kPa in sand lenses — zone verification is non-negotiable.

Process overview

A common error in Dublin anchor design is treating the Boulder Clay as a homogeneous mass and applying uniform bond stress values from standard tables. The till contains random lenses of sand and gravel where bond stress drops sharply. Anchors designed without verifying stratigraphy at each location risk progressive failure under lock-off loads. This is why our technical team insists on correlating anchor borehole data with in-situ permeability tests to confirm the presence of water-bearing layers that could reduce grout quality. For urban excavations near existing structures, the anchor design must also account for strict deflection limits. We integrate excavation monitoring protocols to verify that passive anchors are mobilizing resistance within the design displacement range, ensuring that adjacent buildings on shallow foundations are not affected by unexpected movement.

Local context

Dublin's population exceeds 1.2 million, and much of the city center sits on dense glacial till with a shallow water table. When anchor design underestimates groundwater pressure or ignores boulder obstructions, the consequences escalate quickly. A failed anchor in a deep excavation on the quays can destabilize sheet pile walls, leading to ground loss behind the wall and settlement of historic masonry buildings. The proximity of the River Liffey means tidal fluctuations affect pore pressures within the alluvial deposits, and anchors must be designed for these cyclic conditions. Progressive corrosion in permanent anchors is another risk in Dublin's urban environment, where stray currents from LUAS tram lines can accelerate deterioration if electrical isolation is not properly detailed. A solid anchor design incorporates double corrosion protection and continuity testing to mitigate this threat.

Quick answers

What is the typical cost range for anchor design and testing in Dublin?

Anchor design and testing services in Dublin typically range from €870 for a straightforward single-anchor verification to €3,680 for a comprehensive design package covering multiple anchor types, corrosion protection detailing, and supervised acceptance testing on a medium-sized excavation project.

How does Dublin Boulder Clay affect anchor bond stress values?

Dublin Boulder Clay is an overconsolidated lodgement till with a stiff to hard consistency. In intact till, ultimate bond stresses of 300–450 kPa are achievable with post-grouting techniques. However, the till contains discontinuous sand and gravel lenses — often only 100–300 mm thick — where bond stress can drop below 100 kPa. Each anchor zone must be geologically logged during drilling to confirm the design bond length falls entirely within competent till.

What corrosion protection level is required for permanent anchors in Dublin?

Permanent anchors in Dublin require double corrosion protection (Class II per I.S. EN 1537) due to the urban environment and presence of the LUAS tram system, which introduces stray current corrosion risk. The protection system must include a corrugated plastic sheath over the tendon, cement grout as primary barrier, and a smooth outer sheath with end caps. Electrical isolation testing should be performed on each anchor to verify integrity before lock-off.
